Repple Studio · for the gym

The whole gym.
One board.

Most gym software runs one department well and leaves you to reconcile the rest. Studio holds the timetable, the memberships, the payments, the door, the equipment and the staff in a single record — so the question stops being "what happened in each area" and becomes "which area is moving the others".

Why one board

Nothing in a gym
happens on its own.

A quiet Tuesday class is not a timetable problem. It is a staffing cost, a retention signal and a revenue forecast wearing a timetable's clothes. Take one fact — a member stops coming in — and read it from each part of the business.

Coaching

Their coach sees it first

The pattern breaks in week two and the client rises to the top of their coach's roster, while there is still something to be done about it.

The door

The visit log knows before the till

Attendance used to be countable only where a class was booked and ticked. The door log counts the member who walks in, trains on the floor and leaves — so moving from classes to the floor no longer looks like leaving.

Timetable

A recurring slot frees up

Worth re-timing before the gap becomes the shape of the week. Fill and show are measured per class, so the decision has evidence behind it.

Payroll

Fewer delivered sessions

Which changes what is owed this month — computed from the sessions somebody confirmed, not from the ones that were merely booked.

Revenue

A membership likely to lapse

Visible now, in the register, rather than at renewal when the only remaining option is a discount.

One record

Four views, not four systems

In separate tools these are four unrelated rows nobody joins up until quarter end. Here they are one member, one record, read four ways.

Payroll

Booked and the clock passed
is not the same as delivered.

The easy way to count a trainer's month is to count the bookings whose start time has gone by. It counts no-shows, slots nobody cancelled and sessions the trainer never turned up to — and then it feeds payroll, so the gym pays for sessions that did not happen. Studio will not do it. A session is delivered when somebody recorded that it completed, and a session nobody has marked is reported as unknown rather than rounded in.

  1. 1

    State the policy, once

    Do you pay for a no-show? For a cancellation that came too late to fill the slot? Both are real differences between gyms, so Studio asks rather than assuming, and the answer changes the payable count in front of you.

  2. 2

    The rate is stamped at the moment of marking

    Whatever a session was worth when its outcome was recorded stays with it. Changing your session fee next quarter cannot rewrite what last month cost.

  3. 3

    Settling pays sessions, not periods

    A settlement stamps the exact sessions it covered, so a session marked late simply joins the next run instead of being paid twice — and a session already paid can never be picked up again.

  4. 4

    Unpriced work is reported, not zeroed

    A payable session with no rate against it reads as not priced. The run tells you how many there are and asks you to set a fee, rather than quietly settling them at nothing.

Timetable

Fill and show are
two different questions.

Fill is how full the room was booked. Show is how many of those people turned up. One word for both is how a class reads 71 per cent on one screen and 80 per cent on another. Studio keeps them apart, sums first and divides once, and leaves either as a dash when the figure it needs was never recorded — a class nobody booked has no show rate, which is not the same as everybody failing to turn up.

  • A series is real rows, not a rule — build twelve weeks in one action and then move one week to a different room, or drop the week of a public holiday, without unpicking a recurrence pattern.
  • Skip dates when you build it — hand the series the days the gym is closed and those weeks are simply never created.
  • The roster is a list you tick — at the desk or on the trainer's phone. Only a ticked name counts as having turned up, which is what makes show rate a measurement.
  • A class in the future shows a dash — not a zero. Nought turned up would read as nobody came, rather than as not yet.
  • Class payroll from check-ins — attendance times the per-attendee rate you type. The rate field starts empty, because a figure that belongs to no pay agreement should not be on screen before you have seen what it multiplies.

Memberships & money

Recurring revenue,
by the rules you can check.

Monthly plans count in full. Annual plans are spread across twelve. One-off items — a day pass, a joining fee, a block of personal training — contribute nothing to a monthly figure, because they are not recurring. And if no active membership sits on a priced plan at all, the answer is a dash and a sentence, not a zero.

  • A price book you can audit — retire a plan rather than deleting it, so the memberships sold on it keep pointing somewhere and the history has no holes in it.
  • Money is held in whole units of currency — never as a decimal fraction. Floating point is a rounding error in a spreadsheet and a dispute in a gym.
  • A payment is here because somebody recorded it — never because it was inferred from a membership that ought to have renewed. Card, cash, transfer, direct debit or other, taken at the desk and logged against the member.
  • Freeze, reactivate, cancel — the four states a membership actually has, with the plan it sits on, the day it started and the day it ends, on the same row.
  • Nothing taken is not nothing earned — a period with no payments recorded shows a dash. A gym that has recorded no payments has not necessarily taken none, and the screen refuses to say otherwise.

The door

Every visit,
not just the booked ones.

Attendance used to be countable only where a class was booked and ticked off, which made every member who walks in, trains on the floor and leaves invisible. That under-count reaches further than the headline: retention is read from a break in someone's pattern, so a member who moved from classes to the floor looked exactly like a member who stopped coming.

Dwell

Measured only where it was measured

A visit with no recorded exit has no length — the member may still be inside. It is left out of the average rather than counted as zero minutes, and the screen says how many of the day's visits the average came from.

Anonymous

A walk-in still counts

Check somebody in without attaching a member and the visit is recorded as unattributed — which is true — rather than dropped, which would lose a person your gym actually let in.

Inside now

Who the record believes is here

Everyone still open in the log, with how long they have been in. A visit left open overnight is swept with a note attached, never given a guessed exit time.

Last seen

Days since each member came in

Read from the door log, not from bookings — so it catches the member who quietly moved to the floor and the member who quietly stopped, and tells them apart.

Passes

Drop-ins, guests and class packs

Sold at the desk with a number of visits and an expiry. A pass expiring today is still good today. Guest passes remember which member brought whom.

Unpriced

A pass nobody priced is not free

Pass revenue reads as a dash when no price was recorded, and says how many of the passes it did count. A gym reading "pass revenue: 0" after a week of cash takings makes a worse decision than one reading a dash.

Equipment

A capacity of fourteen
is a claim about the room.

It stops being true the moment six of the rowers break. Without a register, a gym measures its fill rate against a number that quietly became fiction. The equipment register is an inventory that the timetable actually reads: put a class on at a capacity the kit cannot support and Studio says so while you are still typing it.

  • It names the number it can actually seat — "six of eighteen bikes out of action, this class seats twelve, not twenty" — and lets you add the class anyway, because a stale inventory should not stop a class reaching the timetable.
  • An empty register is not an empty gym — if nothing of that kind is recorded, the check returns nothing and says why. It never reports zero, which would tell an owner their class cannot run on the strength of a form nobody filled in.
  • Staff take a machine out of action — from the floor, where they are standing next to it, with a note about what is wrong. Usable units drop immediately and so does the capacity it supports.
  • Retired kit is gone, not broken — it leaves the counts entirely, rather than sitting in the out-of-action pile making the gym look worse than it is.

Bringing your history in

An import that refuses
is doing its job.

Everything Studio does is worthless against an empty record, so your existing spreadsheet has to come with you. The governing rule of the importer is that it never guesses at an ambiguous value. An import that silently reads 03/04/2026 as the third of April when you meant the fourth of March does not fail loudly — it produces a year of plausible, wrong history that nobody notices until a renewal is missed.

  1. 1

    Paste the sheet, header row and all

    Column names are matched against the spellings real exports use — member, customer, client; started, joined, member since; amount, total, gross. Anything it does not recognise is reported to you, never dropped quietly, in case one of them mattered.

  2. 2

    It reads the whole column before it reads a row

    If any date in the file settles the convention — a day above the twelfth can only be a day — that settles it for the file. If every date works read either way round, it stops and asks you which, rather than moving somebody's renewal by nine months.

  3. 3

    You see what would happen before anything happens

    How many rows are ready, how many need attention, which lines they are and what is wrong with each. Nothing is written until you say so.

  4. 4

    And it tells you plainly what it did

    All of them, none of them, or the exact split — including the warning that the rows which did land are saved, so paste only the fixed ones back or you will import them twice.

What the sheet saysWhat the importer does, and why
A quarterly plan Refused with a reason. A price book holds monthly, yearly and one-off, and there is nowhere truthful to put a quarter. Calling it monthly would divide your recurring revenue by three; calling it yearly would multiply it by four.
A plan with a blank price Refused. A blank price is an unfinished row, not a free plan. A deliberate zero is accepted, because a complimentary or staff membership is a real thing a gym sells at nothing on purpose.
A status it does not recognise Refused rather than defaulted to active. A cancelled member imported as active gets chased for money.
An amount with four decimal places Refused rather than rounded. A column of four-decimal figures is a unit price or an exchange rate, and rounding it silently turns a data problem into a money problem.
A negative payment Refused — that is a refund, and this is not the tool for one. A zero is accepted, because a comped month and a correction both really happen.
The same email twice The first row keeps its place and the second is flagged against the line number of the first. Two members for one person is not a thing you want to discover later.
A membership ending before it starts Refused. So is the thirty-first of February, which a date library would otherwise roll helpfully forward into March.
A payment with no name and no email Refused — it cannot be attributed to anybody. A payment whose member simply cannot be matched is still recorded, as unattributed, because that is true and dropping it would lose money you actually took.

Payments and plans import in full. Member rows can be checked but not yet imported — a membership has to point at a real Repple account, and creating one is an invitation rather than a row we can insert. Running the check is still most of the work of cleaning an export: it finds the bad dates, the unreadable amounts and the columns nobody will recognise.

Data protection

Somebody asked to be
erased. There is a clock.

The store listing tells your members their account is deleted within thirty days of asking. The clock starts the moment they tap it in the app, not when you next open the console — so the queue exists, it counts down, and it goes red when a request is past the promise.

  • Two confirmations, and both say what goes — the profile, the workouts, the logs, the scans, the messages and the bookings, across every table that holds them. Their invoices and memberships go too.
  • What stays, and why — payments, door-log visits and guest passes remain, with the person detached from them. Your books do not develop a hole because somebody exercised their rights.
  • The erasure is logged even though the person is not — that they asked, and when you actioned it, is kept. The record of the deletion is the evidence that it happened.
  • A failed read is never an all-clear — if the queue cannot be loaded the screen says so in as many words, because "we could not check" and "nobody is waiting" are different answers and only one of them is safe.

On the desk, not just the phone

Some jobs need
a proper screen.

Studio runs on a phone for the floor and in a browser for the office. Reconciling a month, restructuring a timetable or working through an import is work for a keyboard and a wide table. Both call the same functions through the same permissions — there is no second version of the truth.

Staff

A trainer carrying clients
and running no sessions.

That is the signal an owner wants, and it is invisible on a headcount. Studio scores a trainer on the only two things a gym can honestly observe — how many clients they carry, and how many sessions they actually delivered — and it says which of the two is the problem in a sentence.

  • Client load and delivery weigh the same — a full book is a dozen clients and twenty sessions a month, not a ceiling on how good anybody is.
  • Idle sits outside the ranking — a brand-new trainer with nobody yet is not doing badly, and the roster refuses to call them that.
  • The exposure, not the headcount — alongside how many trainers are flagged, Studio reports how many clients are sitting with them. One flagged trainer with thirty clients is not a small problem.
  • Never a figure about what they pay Repple — an owner's dashboard shows the gym, not somebody's subscription. What a trainer's work is worth is priced at your fee, and only once every session has an outcome.

Your figures against your records

Two sources for one number,
and nothing comparing them.

The financial-health review works from figures you type — revenue, expenses, members, joiners and leavers — and it is honest that it is doing so. But your register already knows two of them: memberships on priced plans give recurring revenue, and memberships marked active give the member count.

Studio does not pick a winner. An owner typing a different number is usually right about something the records do not hold — a corporate contract invoiced offline, a price that changed mid-month — and silently overwriting that would replace one wrong number with another and lose what you know in the process. So it names both figures, says how far apart they are, and leaves the decision with you.

Straight about limits

The parts that are not
built yet, said out loud.

A gym choosing an operating record deserves to know where the edges are before the migration, not after it. These are the ones that would otherwise be discovered in month two.

Members

A membership needs a Repple account

A membership belongs to a person, not to a row of spreadsheet text. Somebody who has never opened Repple cannot be given one yet, and member rows in a CSV are validated rather than imported. The screen says so instead of failing quietly.

Accounting

Not connected

There is a place for a ledger integration and it is not switched on. That is why the cash position on the overview is permanently a dash with the words "connect accounting" under it, rather than a number nobody can source.

Notifications

Queued, not delivered

Push a promotion to your members and Studio reports how many it queued. Whether a notice arrives depends on each member's phone, so nothing here claims a delivery it cannot see.

Announcements

A note to yourself, for now

The announcement box saves to your device and does not reach your trainers yet. The placeholder in the field says exactly that, so nobody writes an important message into it believing otherwise.

Redemptions

Promo codes are not counted

A promotion shows its code and its discount and nothing else, because nothing in the system increments a redemption count. A number that never moves is worse than no number.

Lifetime value

No churn signal, no LTV

A lifetime-value figure needs a measured lifespan. Until there is one, Studio does not print an invented multiplier and call it analysis — it says the figure is not available.

Control

Your gym's data,
fenced off properly.

Rows
Isolated at the database. Every table carries row-level rules keyed to your gym. Another gym's account cannot reach your rows even if its app asked for them.
Roles
Owner, trainer, member. Staff see their own clients and rota; owners see the business; nobody sees private coaching messages. The console enforces it page by page.
Export
Yours to take. Anyone with an account can export everything Repple holds about them as a file, from Settings, without asking a person for it.
Brand
Your colours. Set the name and the palette and it carries through what your members and coaches see, with a live preview before you commit to it.

Straight answers

The questions worth asking before you move a gym's operating record into something new.

Can we bring our existing members and history across?

Payments and membership plans import from a pasted spreadsheet in full, with a preview first and a reason attached to every row it refuses. Member rows can be checked but not yet written, because a membership has to point at a real Repple account — so the members themselves join by invitation. Running the member check anyway is worth it: it finds the bad dates, the unreadable amounts and the columns nobody will recognise.

Do our trainers have to use it for payroll to work?

Somebody has to record what happened to each session — the trainer in Repple Coach, or you in the console. That is the whole point: the alternative is inferring delivery from the clock, which is how a gym ends up paying for no-shows. Until every session in a period has an outcome, the payroll figure tells you it is not final and names how many are outstanding.

What happens if a figure cannot be worked out?

You get a dash and a sentence saying what is missing — no session fee set, no priced plan on an active membership, no capacity recorded against these classes, no exits logged so no average stay. A confident number from a thin record is the most expensive thing a business tool can hand you.

Who can see what?

Access is enforced by row-level rules in the database rather than by what a screen chooses to render. Your gym's rows are unreachable from another gym's account. Trainers see their own clients and rota; you see the business; nobody sees the private messages between a coach and a client. The detail is in Privacy.

Does it replace our door system?

It records visits — at the desk, from a member's entry barcode, or as an unattributed walk-in — and that log is what attendance, dwell time and last-seen are read from. It is a record of who came in, not a lock on the turnstile.
